\begin{abstract}
Direct-gap III--V semiconductors are the material basis of nano-photonic light
emitters, and their design---band-gap engineering for a target wavelength and
heterostructure engineering for efficiency---requires a device simulator that
couples carrier transport to radiative recombination and optical output. We
present an open-source, two-dimensional finite-element drift-diffusion (DD) solver
that adds a bimolecular (radiative) recombination channel and an optical
post-processor reporting the internal quantum efficiency (IQE), emission
wavelength and optical power, together with heterojunction band offsets
(Anderson's rule), a self-consistent Schr\"odinger--Poisson quantum correction,
and Fermi--Dirac statistics for degenerate cladding. For a GaAs homojunction LED
the solver reproduces the analytic built-in potential ($1.151$~V, numeric $=$
analytic) and an emission wavelength of $873$~nm set by the $1.42$~eV gap, with a
peak IQE of $0.53$. Sweeping the active material tunes the emission from the
near-infrared (GaAs, $873$~nm) through the red
(Al\textsubscript{0.3}Ga\textsubscript{0.7}As, $689$~nm) to the ultraviolet
(GaN, $366$~nm), following the material band gap. A GaAs/AlGaAs
double-heterostructure (DH) LED confines both carrier types in the narrow-gap
active region; the resulting overlap raises the peak IQE from $0.53$
(homojunction) to $0.90$, quantifying the classic DH efficiency advantage on a
single, self-consistent transport model. The tool provides a compact, physically
transparent and reproducible platform for the design of nano-scale direct-gap
emitters.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{L}{ight-emitting} diodes (LEDs) built from direct-gap III--V
semiconductors are central to solid-state lighting, displays, optical interconnect
and sensing, and their nanoscale active regions---thin double heterostructures and
quantum wells---are the emerging materials of advanced photonic
devices~\cite{schubert,alferov}. Two engineering levers dominate their design: the
choice of active material, which sets the emission wavelength through the band gap,
and the heterostructure, which confines injected carriers to raise the radiative
efficiency~\cite{kroemer,caseypanish}. Predictive design therefore needs a device
model that couples electrical transport to the radiative process and to the
optical output, so that current, efficiency and emission spectrum are computed
consistently from one self-consistent solution.

The drift-diffusion (DD) framework~\cite{selberherr,vanroosbroeck} is the standard
basis for such modelling. To describe an emitter it must be augmented with (i) a
radiative (bimolecular) recombination channel that both consumes carriers and
produces photons, (ii) heterojunction band-edge offsets so that a narrow-gap
active region clad by a wider-gap material actually confines carriers, and
(iii)---for the thin, degenerately-doped layers of a real device---quantum-confinement
and Fermi--Dirac corrections. This paper presents a compact, open-source
finite-element solver that integrates all of these, and uses it to characterise
wavelength tuning across III--V materials and the efficiency gain of a
double-heterostructure emitter. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews the relevant
literature; Section~\ref{sec:gap} states the research gap and contributions;
Section~\ref{sec:model} details the device model; Section~\ref{sec:results}
presents the results; and Section~\ref{sec:disc} discusses limitations and future
work.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Heterostructure emitters and their material basis}
The double heterostructure (DH), proposed by Kroemer~\cite{kroemer} and Alferov and
developed for lasers by Casey and Panish~\cite{caseypanish,alferov}, is the
foundational concept of efficient III--V emitters: a narrow-gap active layer clad
by wider-gap barriers confines both carrier types to a common region, maximising
their overlap and hence the radiative rate. Anderson's electron-affinity
rule~\cite{anderson} provides the first-order band-edge offsets that create this
confinement. The material-parameter basis for quantitative modelling of these
alloys---band gaps, affinities, effective masses and their composition
dependence---was consolidated by Vurgaftman \emph{et al.}~\cite{vurgaftman}, and
the device physics is treated comprehensively by Sze and Ng~\cite{sze} and
Schubert~\cite{schubert}. The nitride system enabled efficient blue and
ultraviolet emission~\cite{nakamura}, extending the accessible spectral range that
we exercise here from the near-infrared to the UV.

\subsection{Recombination models for LEDs}
LED efficiency is governed by the competition between radiative and non-radiative
recombination. Non-radiative Shockley--Read--Hall (SRH) recombination through deep
traps~\cite{shockley,hall} dominates at low injection; the radiative
(bimolecular) rate $R_{\mathrm{rad}}=B(np-n_i^2)$ scales with the carrier product
and overtakes SRH at higher injection; and Auger recombination, cubic in carrier
density, limits efficiency at the highest injection. This hierarchy is often
summarised by the phenomenological ``ABC'' rate
$R=An+Bn^2+Cn^3$~\cite{karpov}, whose internal-quantum-efficiency droop at high
current has been studied extensively for nitride
LEDs~\cite{piprek_droop,karpov}. Measured radiative coefficients $B$ for III--V
materials underpin such models~\cite{olshansky}. A physical device simulator must
represent each channel with the correct carrier dependence \emph{and} its exact
derivatives, so that the coupled nonlinear solve converges and the efficiency is
predicted rather than fitted.

\subsection{Optoelectronic device simulation}
Coupling carrier transport to optics is the domain of optoelectronic device
simulators, surveyed by Piprek~\cite{piprek_book} and Chuang~\cite{chuang}.
Commercial and research tools (e.g. Crosslight, Synopsys, and academic
drift-diffusion--optics codes~\cite{bulashevich,grupen}) solve DD together with
recombination and, for lasers, optical-mode and gain models. These tools are
powerful but typically closed, and their generality can obscure the physics for
teaching and rapid design studies. Finite-element DD solvers handle the
non-rectangular geometries of real emitters naturally, yet compact, open,
readable implementations that also return the optical figures of merit
(IQE, emission wavelength, optical power) \emph{consistently} with the electrical
current are comparatively scarce.

\subsection{Quantum and degeneracy corrections in thin active regions}
Modern emitters use active regions only a few nanometres thick and cladding doped
to degeneracy. Accurate modelling therefore benefits from a self-consistent
Schr\"odinger--Poisson treatment of the confined states~\cite{trellakis} and from
Fermi--Dirac statistics in the degenerate layers~\cite{sze}. Including these on the
\emph{same} transport mesh as the recombination and optical models---rather than as
separate post-processing---keeps the electrical and optical outputs mutually
consistent, which is the design philosophy of the present solver.

\section{Research Gap and Contributions}
\label{sec:gap}
The literature reveals a gap between two extremes. On one side, compact analytic
and ``ABC'' models~\cite{karpov} predict efficiency trends but abstract away the
device electrostatics, carrier confinement and spatial recombination profile. On
the other, full commercial optoelectronic simulators~\cite{piprek_book} capture
the physics but are closed, heavy, and not readily inspectable or reproducible for
research and teaching. What is missing is a \emph{compact, open-source,
finite-element drift-diffusion} solver that (i) integrates SRH, Auger and
radiative recombination with exact analytic derivatives so the coupled Newton
solve converges; (ii) returns the optical figures of merit \emph{current-consistently}
($qR_{\mathrm{rad}}$ equal to the radiative part of the terminal current by
construction); and (iii) supports heterojunctions, Schr\"odinger--Poisson
confinement and Fermi--Dirac statistics on the same mesh, so a simple homojunction
and a fully-corrected DH quantum device share one code path.

\noindent\textbf{How this work fills the gap.} We contribute:
\begin{itemize}
\item a 2-D finite-element DD solver with a bimolecular radiative channel and an
optical post-processor (IQE, wavelength, optical power) that is current-consistent
by construction (Section~\ref{sec:rad});
\item heterojunction electrostatics (Anderson offsets), a self-consistent
Schr\"odinger--Poisson correction and Fermi--Dirac statistics on the same mesh,
each opt-in and vanishing in its bulk limit (Section~\ref{sec:hetero});
\item validated GaAs LED characteristics, and demonstrations of material-tuned
emission from the near-infrared to the ultraviolet and of the
double-heterostructure efficiency advantage (Section~\ref{sec:results}); and
\item an open, reproducible implementation (MIT licence) with scripts that
regenerate every figure.
\end{itemize}

\section{Device Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Drift-diffusion core}
The steady state is solved in the normalised quasi-Fermi-potential variables
$u=\psi/V_T$, $v=\phi_n/V_T$, $w=\phi_p/V_T$ (De~Mari scaling~\cite{demari}), with
$n=n_ie^{\,u-v}$, $p=n_ie^{\,w-u}$. Poisson's equation and the two carrier
continuity equations are discretised with linear (P1) triangular finite elements
and assembled into a $3N\times3N$ system solved by a fully-coupled damped Newton
method using an exact analytic Jacobian. The device geometry is meshed with
linear triangles, either by a built-in structured generator or by an external
mesh~\cite{gmsh}, so non-rectangular emitter cross-sections are handled naturally.
Terminal currents are extracted as the sum of the natural continuity residuals
over a contact's nodes, which is conservative by construction. A thermal-equilibrium solve seeds the bias sweep,
and each forward-bias point warm-starts from the previous solution---necessary
because the direct-gap built-in potentials exceed $1$~V.

\subsection{Radiative recombination and optical output}
\label{sec:rad}
The light-emitting mechanism of a direct-gap diode is radiative (bimolecular)
recombination, added as a net-recombination term
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{rad}}=B\,(np-n_i^2),
\end{equation}
where $B$ (m\textsuperscript{3}\,s\textsuperscript{-1}) is the material radiative
coefficient~\cite{olshansky}. Because of the $(np-n_i^2)$ prefactor the rate
vanishes at equilibrium and leaves it unchanged; its exact derivatives
$\partial R/\partial n=Bp$, $\partial R/\partial p=Bn$ enter the analytic Jacobian,
preserving quadratic convergence. Shockley--Read--Hall~\cite{shockley,hall} and
Auger channels are included as competing non-radiative paths, together realising
the ABC hierarchy~\cite{karpov} from first principles rather than as a fit. After a
converged forward-bias solve the optical post-processor integrates the radiative
rate over the device (on the same quadrature and corrected densities used in the
assembly) and reports
\begin{align}
I_{\mathrm{rad}} &= q\,R_{\mathrm{rad}}, &
\mathrm{IQE} &= \frac{R_{\mathrm{rad}}}{R_{\mathrm{rad}}+R_{\mathrm{SRH}}+R_{\mathrm{Auger}}},\\
E_{\mathrm{ph}} &= E_g^{\mathrm{active}}, &
\lambda &= \frac{hc}{E_{\mathrm{ph}}}, \quad P_{\mathrm{opt}}=R_{\mathrm{rad}}E_{\mathrm{ph}}.
\end{align}
By construction $q R_{\mathrm{rad}}$ equals the radiative part of the terminal
current, so the electrical and optical outputs are mutually consistent---the key
property that distinguishes this coupling from post-hoc optical estimates. The
radiative coefficient is significant only in direct-gap materials; the bundled
direct-gap files (GaAs, AlGaAs, GaN) carry representative $300$~K values, and a
guardrail warns if radiative emission is requested on an indirect material.

\subsection{Heterojunction, quantum and Fermi--Dirac corrections}
\label{sec:hetero}
Multi-material devices are solved with a heterojunction electrostatics core:
position-dependent permittivity and mobility, and band-edge offsets
$\Delta E_c,\Delta E_v$ from Anderson's electron-affinity rule~\cite{anderson} that
appear as fixed additive shifts in the carrier-density exponents. A narrow-gap
active region clad by a wider-gap material therefore presents potential barriers
that confine both carrier types. For thin bodies a self-consistent
Schr\"odinger--Poisson correction~\cite{trellakis} quantises the transverse states
into subbands and enters transport as a charge-conserving additive quantum
potential; for degenerately-doped cladding, Fermi--Dirac statistics~\cite{sze}
enter as a degeneracy shift refreshed by the same outer self-consistency loop. All
corrections vanish in their respective non-degenerate, bulk limits, recovering the
classical Boltzmann solver exactly, so a single code path spans the homojunction
and the fully-corrected DH quantum device.

\subsection{GaAs LED: validation and characteristics}
A GaAs $p$--$n$ homojunction ($N_A=N_D=10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $E_g=1.42$~eV,
$n_i=2.15\times10^6\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, $B=2\times10^{-16}\,\mathrm{m^3 s^{-1}}$)
gives a numerical built-in potential of $1.151$~V, matching the analytic
$V_{bi}=V_T\ln(N_AN_D/n_i^2)=1.151$~V. Fig.~\ref{fig:gaasiv} shows the forward
$I$--$V$ together with the radiative component $I_{\mathrm{rad}}=qR_{\mathrm{rad}}$;
at $1.3$~V the device carries $53.7$~$\mu$A total with $1.68$~$\mu$A radiative and
$2.4$~$\mu$W of optical output. The emission wavelength is $873$~nm
($E_{\mathrm{ph}}=1.42$~eV), the expected near-infrared line of GaAs. The internal
quantum efficiency (Fig.~\ref{fig:gaasiqe}) rises with injection as the
bimolecular rate ($\propto np$) overtakes the SRH channel, reaching a peak of
$0.53$---the characteristic low-to-high-injection efficiency turn-on of the ABC
picture, here emerging directly from the coupled solve.

\subsection{Material-tuned emission}
Keeping the device geometry fixed and changing only the active material tunes the
emission wavelength through the band gap (Fig.~\ref{fig:tuning},
Table~\ref{tab:tuning}): GaAs emits at $873$~nm (near-infrared),
Al\textsubscript{0.3}Ga\textsubscript{0.7}As at $689$~nm (red), and GaN at
$366$~nm (ultraviolet). The solver converges the full electrical problem for each
material---spanning intrinsic densities from $n_i\sim10^6\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ (GaAs)
down to the extremely small $n_i$ of wide-gap GaN, a numerically demanding
range---and returns the emission line directly from the active-region gap. This
spans the technologically important range from optical-communication wavelengths
to visible and UV emitters on a single, consistent model, and the values agree
with the accepted band gaps of these alloys~\cite{vurgaftman,nakamura}.

\subsection{Double-heterostructure efficiency advantage}
Fig.~\ref{fig:dhconf} shows the carrier densities along the transport direction of
a GaAs/AlGaAs double-heterostructure (DH) LED: a $100$~nm GaAs active region
($\sim\!10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$) clad on both sides by degenerately-doped
($\sim\!10^{18}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$) AlGaAs, with a $10$~nm body solved with the
heterojunction, Schr\"odinger--Poisson and Fermi--Dirac options all enabled. The
Anderson band offsets confine \emph{both} electrons and holes to the narrow-gap
active region, where their densities rise by orders of magnitude and overlap---the
physical basis of efficient recombination in a real DH emitter~\cite{kroemer,
caseypanish}. The device built-in potential is $1.766$~V and the emission remains
at $873$~nm, correctly originating from the GaAs active region despite the AlGaAs
cladding.

The efficiency consequence is quantified in Fig.~\ref{fig:dhiqe}: confining the
carriers raises the peak IQE to $0.90$, compared with $0.53$ for the GaAs
homojunction of Fig.~\ref{fig:gaasiqe}---the classic double-heterostructure
advantage, reproduced end-to-end on a single self-consistent transport model. The
same framework thus captures both design levers of a nano-photonic emitter:
wavelength through the active material and efficiency through the heterostructure.

\subsection{Numerical robustness, consistency and cost}
The coupled solve is robust across the full operating range. Each direct-gap LED
must be ramped from equilibrium through its $>\!1$~V built-in potential by bias
continuation, because a direct jump into forward bias would overflow the
exponential density terms; per-step damping then keeps the Newton iteration
bounded. Warm-starting each bias point from the previous solution, the GaAs
homojunction sweep of $27$ points completes in under $3$~s on a single core, while
the fully-corrected DH device---heterojunction, Schr\"odinger--Poisson and
Fermi--Dirac all active, $1037$ nodes / $1920$ triangles---completes its $16$-point
sweep in $39$~s, the added cost coming from the outer self-consistency loop that
refreshes the quantum and degeneracy shifts. Crucially, the optical output is
consistent with the electrical solution by construction: because the radiative
rate scale is exactly the terminal-current group divided by $q$, the reported
$I_{\mathrm{rad}}=qR_{\mathrm{rad}}$ is the radiative part of the terminal current
to machine precision, and the IQE therefore never exceeds unity. The efficiency
curves span more than four orders of magnitude of drive current with no loss of
convergence, a direct benefit of retaining the exact analytic Jacobian for every
recombination channel.

\subsection{Discussion}
Three features make the model suitable for nano-photonic device design. First, the
radiative rate shares the terminal-current scale, so $qR_{\mathrm{rad}}$ is exactly
the radiative part of the current and the optical output cannot drift from the
electrical solution. Second, all corrections are opt-in and vanish in their bulk
limits, so a single code path spans a simple homojunction and a fully-corrected DH
quantum device. Third, the analytic Jacobian is preserved by every recombination
channel and correction, keeping the coupled solve robust across the more than four
orders of magnitude of current spanned by the efficiency curves.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:disc}
Several limitations bound the present model and define its roadmap. (i) The optical
model reports the \emph{internal} quantum efficiency and the active-gap emission
\emph{line}; light-extraction efficiency, the full emission \emph{spectrum}
(thermal and compositional broadening) and photon recycling are not modelled, so
absolute optical power and spectral width are indicative rather than
predictive. (ii) The radiative coefficient $B$ is taken as a constant $300$~K
material value; its temperature and carrier-density dependence, together with the
temperature dependence of the band gap~\cite{varshni} and self-heating, are
neglected, so the model is isothermal. (iii) Emission is assigned to the active-region bulk gap; quantum-well
active regions with confined-state transitions and a blue-shifted, subband-resolved
emission energy require the $k\!\cdot\!p$ subband structure, which the present
single-band correction does not resolve. (iv) The Auger coefficient and the
resulting high-injection efficiency droop~\cite{piprek_droop} depend on material
parameters that should be calibrated against measurement for quantitative droop
prediction. (v) Transport is classical drift-diffusion, so tunnelling injection
and ballistic effects in ultra-thin barriers are outside scope. Planned extensions
include a spectral/extraction optical model, multi-quantum-well active regions
with $k\!\cdot\!p$ subbands, temperature-dependent parameters with self-heating,
and calibration of the ABC coefficients against measured LED data.

\section{Conclusion}
We have presented an open-source finite-element drift-diffusion solver for
direct-gap III--V light-emitting diodes that couples carrier transport to
bimolecular radiative recombination and a current-consistent optical
post-processor, with heterojunction, Schr\"odinger--Poisson and Fermi--Dirac
corrections on the same mesh---filling the gap between compact ABC efficiency
models and closed commercial optoelectronic simulators. The GaAs LED
characteristics are validated against analytic physics; material selection tunes
the emission from $873$~nm (GaAs) to $366$~nm (GaN); and a GaAs/AlGaAs double
heterostructure raises the peak internal quantum efficiency from $0.53$ to $0.90$
by confining carriers to the active region. The tool is a compact, transparent and
reproducible platform for the design of nanoscale direct-gap emitters.
